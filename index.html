Stigmas and preconceptions

Nine things people believe about the market

Some are flat wrong. Some are half-true in ways that matter. Pretending the half-true ones are myths is how people get hurt, so they're marked honestly.

"Investing is just gambling."

Half-true, and the half matters. In a casino the odds are fixed against you and the total pot never grows — one person's win is another's loss. Owning shares in companies is different: those businesses actually make things and earn profits, so the pot itself can grow over time. But how you invest decides which one you're doing. Buying a spread of hundreds of companies and holding for twenty years is ownership. Buying a coin your friend hyped on Friday and selling Monday is gambling with extra steps. Same market, completely different activity.

"You need thousands of pounds to start."

Not any more. Fractional shares let you buy a slice of a share, so a company trading at £400 is reachable with £10. Plenty of regulated platforms have no minimum. The real barrier isn't the sum, it's that small amounts feel pointless — which is exactly the intuition the chart on the first tab is designed to break.

"It's for rich old men in suits."

It was, and the image stuck. For most of its history the market genuinely was gated by brokers, phone calls and high fees. That changed within living memory. What hasn't fully changed is who feels welcome — which is a marketing and education problem, not a rule. The mechanics do not check your age or your postcode.

"You could lose everything overnight."

True for one company, misleading for a market. A single business can go bankrupt and take your entire stake with it — this is real and it happens. A fund holding hundreds or thousands of companies across many countries has never gone to zero, because that would require every one of them failing at once. The way people actually lose everything is by putting it all in one thing, borrowing to invest, or handing it to a fraud.

"It's rigged against ordinary people."

Partly, but not in the way it's usually meant. Professionals genuinely have faster data, cheaper trading and better information than you. If your plan is to out-trade them minute by minute, you will lose. What they can't take from you is time — they answer to clients who want results this quarter, and you don't. A cheap index fund — a single fund that simply buys every company in a market rather than picking — held for thirty years has beaten most professional fund managers over the same period, and it requires no skill at all.

"You need to be good at maths."

No. The arithmetic is addition, percentages and patience. Nobel-winning economists have picked bad investments and disciplined people with no qualifications have done fine. Temperament beats cleverness here more reliably than in almost any other field.

"I'm too young to think about this."

Backwards. Being young is the single largest advantage available in investing, and it's the only one that can't be bought. Every year you wait is a year removed from the far end of the curve, where the growth is steepest. A 20-year-old investing for ten years and then stopping entirely can end up ahead of a 35-year-old who pays in for thirty. Time does more work than money.

"Investing is basically a get-rich-quick thing."

The opposite, and the confusion is deliberate. Real investing is slow enough to be boring. The people presenting it as fast are usually selling a course, a signals group, or a coin they already own. Ask why someone with a genuine money-printing method would spend their time recruiting strangers.

"If I don't understand it, I shouldn't touch it."

Actually a good instinct — keep it. This is the one preconception worth holding on to. Not understanding something is an excellent reason not to put money in it. The mistake is concluding you can't understand it, when the truth is nobody taught you. That's a curriculum failure, not a limit on you.

Why the stigma exists at all

Three things feed it. Films and news only cover markets when they explode, so the crashes are famous and the boring decades aren't. Social media rewards whoever posts the most extreme number, which means the loudest voices are structurally the least reliable. And schools mostly skip it, so people arrive at adulthood with the film version and nothing to correct it.

None of that makes the market safe. It just means the fear people carry is shaped by the most dramatic stories rather than the most common ones.

History of the market

Four hundred years of the same mistake

Read the timeline and a pattern shows up: a new thing appears, people decide it can only go up, it doesn't, everyone swears never again, and roughly one generation later it happens again. Milestones have blue dots; falls are marked in red and labelled “crash”.

1602

The first company anyone could own

The Dutch East India Company needed more money than any single group of merchants could raise, so it sold shares to the public in Amsterdam — and crucially, let people resell them to each other. That resale market is the ancestor of every exchange on the Markets tab.

1637 Crash

Tulip mania

Dutch tulip bulb contracts reached extraordinary prices before collapsing. Historians argue about how widespread the damage really was, but it survives as the original example of people buying something purely because the price was rising.

1698

London starts in a coffee house

Brokers were reportedly thrown out of the Royal Exchange for being rowdy, so they did business in Jonathan's Coffee House, where John Castaing posted a list of stock and commodity prices. The London Stock Exchange formalised in 1801.

1720 Crash

The South Sea Bubble

A company with monopoly trading rights and very little actual trade saw its shares soar and collapse within a year. Isaac Newton lost a large sum, and is supposed to have said he could calculate the motion of the stars but not the madness of people.

1792

Wall Street, under a tree

Twenty-four brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement in New York, setting commission rates and agreeing to trade with each other first. It grew into the New York Stock Exchange.

1887

Johannesburg opens for gold

Gold was found on the Witwatersrand in 1886, creating a sudden need to fund mining companies, and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange opened on 8 November the following year. It is now the largest exchange in Africa.

1896

Someone invents the market average

Charles Dow published an average of 12 industrial stocks so people could describe "the market" in one number. The Dow Jones Industrial Average now holds 30 companies and is still quoted daily, despite a calculation method most economists consider outdated.

1929 Crash

The Wall Street Crash

After a decade of rising prices and heavy borrowing to buy shares, the market fell over several days in October and kept falling for years. It contributed to the Great Depression, and the US market did not return to its 1929 peak until 1954. Every later fear of the stock market is descended from this one.

1971

The market leaves the trading floor

Nasdaq launched as the world's first electronic stock market — no shouting, no paper, just a network showing quotes. Almost all trading today is electronic, which is why exchange floors now look like television sets.

1987 Crash

Black Monday

On 19 October the Dow fell about 23% in a single day — still its worst one-day percentage drop. Automated selling programs are widely blamed for accelerating it. Notably, the US market finished the year roughly flat.

2000 Crash

The dot-com bubble

Internet companies with no profits, and sometimes no product, reached enormous valuations. The Nasdaq peaked in March 2000 and lost around three-quarters of its value over the next two and a half years. Some of the survivors — Amazon among them — became the biggest companies on earth, which is why "the technology was real" and "the price was insane" can both be true at once.

2008 Crash

The global financial crisis

Mortgages — long-term loans used to buy a house — given to people who couldn't repay them were bundled into products that banks misjudged as safe. When US house prices fell the damage spread through the whole financial system, Lehman Brothers collapsed in September, and markets fell worldwide. This is why banks now face far stricter rules on how much of their own money they must hold.

2020 Crash

The Covid crash

In February and March markets fell faster than in any previous crash on record as countries locked down. They then recovered unusually quickly, helped by enormous government and central bank support. Anyone who sold at the bottom locked in the loss; anyone who did nothing was back to even within months.

2021

GameStop and the meme stock

Ordinary individual investors — "retail" investors, as opposed to professional firms — coordinating on Reddit drove up shares in a struggling video game shop, causing heavy losses for professional funds betting against it. It was genuinely a moment of ordinary people moving a market — and also a moment where many latecomers bought at the top and lost badly. Both halves of that sentence are the lesson.

What the timeline is actually showing

Crashes are normal features of markets, not malfunctions. Roughly speaking, a fall of 10% or more has historically shown up about once a year, and a fall of 20% or more about every three and a half years — there have been 27 of those since 1928. Every one of them felt like the end at the time, and every one of them is a small notch on a long-term chart. That's not a promise the next one recovers — it's a reason not to build a plan that assumes calm.

Definitions and an introduction

Stocks and shares

If you read only one tab, read this one. Everything else on the site is built on what follows.

What you're actually buying

A company that wants to grow needs money. It can borrow it — that's the Bonds tab — or it can sell off pieces of itself. Those pieces are shares. Buy one and you are a part-owner of that business. Not a customer, not a lender: an owner, with a claim on its profits and, usually, a vote at its annual meeting.

"Stock" and "shares" are used almost interchangeably. Strictly, stock is the general concept of company ownership and a share is one unit of it. Nobody will correct you.

Where shares come from, and where you buy them

When a private company first sells shares to the public it's called an IPO — an initial public offering. That's the primary market, and the money goes to the company. After that, shares change hands between investors on the secondary market, which is what people mean by "the stock market". Buying Apple shares today sends your money to whoever sold them, not to Apple.

This surprises people, and it's worth sitting with: the daily market is mostly investors trading with each other over the price of things that already exist.

How the price gets set

There's no official price. At any moment there's a highest price someone will pay — the bid — and a lowest price someone will accept — the ask. The gap between them is the spread, and it's a hidden cost you pay on every trade. A trade happens when the two meet, and that becomes "the price" until the next one.

So a share price isn't a measure of what a company is worth. It's a live tally of what people currently believe about its future — which is why prices jump on rumours and barely move on good results that everyone already expected.

The mistake almost every beginner makes

A £3 share is not "cheaper" than a £900 share. Price per share depends entirely on how many shares exist. What matters is market capitalisation — share price multiplied by the number of shares — which is the value of the whole company. A company can split one £900 share into nine £100 shares and nothing has changed, exactly like getting change for a note. If you take one thing from this tab, take this one.

The two ways shares make you money

Capital growth
The share is worth more than you paid, and you sell. Until you sell it's a paper gain — real enough to see, not real enough to spend.
Dividends
The company hands out part of its profit to shareholders while you keep holding. This has its own tab, because it's where compounding really lives.

Two order types worth knowing before you click anything

Market order
"Buy it now at whatever the going price is." Fast, and occasionally expensive in a fast-moving or thinly traded stock.
Limit order
"Buy it only at £42 or less." You control the price, but it may never fill. Generally the safer habit.

Funds: the thing most people should understand first

Picking individual companies is the version of investing that television shows you, and it's the hardest. The alternative is a fund: a single purchase that spreads your money across many companies at once.

An index fund or ETF tracks a whole market automatically — buy one that follows the S&P 500 and you own a sliver of 500 large American companies. No stock picking, very low fees, and if one company collapses it's a scratch rather than a wound. Over long periods, most professional fund managers charging you to pick cleverly have failed to beat exactly this. It's the least exciting product in finance, and it barely existed when your grandparents were your age.

Getting paid to own things

Dividends

A dividend is a share of a company's profit, paid to its owners. If you own the shares, you are one of the owners. It arrives whether or not you do anything.

Why companies pay them

A company that makes a profit has a choice: reinvest it in growing the business, or hand some to shareholders. Young, fast-growing companies usually reinvest everything and pay nothing — Amazon has never paid a dividend in its entire history as a public company. Mature companies in steady industries like utilities, banks and supermarkets tend to pay regularly, because they have more cash than they have obvious ways to grow.

Neither approach is better. A company paying no dividend isn't stingy, and one paying a large dividend isn't generous — they're at different points in their life.

The one number people quote

Dividend yield is the annual dividend divided by the share price, as a percentage. A £40 share paying £2 a year yields 5%.

Notice what that fraction does. If the share price falls and the dividend stays put, the yield goes up. So an unusually high yield is often not a generous company — it's a company whose share price has collapsed because the market expects the dividend to be cut. This is called a yield trap, and chasing it is one of the most common ways new investors walk directly into a falling business.

A better second number

The payout ratio is the proportion of profit being paid out as dividends. A company paying out 40% of its profits has room to keep paying if things get worse. A company paying out 110% is paying you with borrowed money or savings, and that cannot continue. Yield tells you what you get; payout ratio tells you whether you'll keep getting it.

The four dates, and the one that confuses everyone

1

Declaration date

The company announces it's paying a dividend, how much, and when.

2

Ex-dividend date

The cut-off. Buy on or after this date and you do not get this payment — the seller does. This is the one that catches people out.

3

Record date

The company checks its list of who owns shares and therefore who gets paid.

4

Payment date

The money lands in your account.

There is no free dividend

On the ex-dividend date a share price typically drops by roughly the amount of the dividend, because the company is about to be that much poorer in cash. So buying a share the day before a big dividend to "catch" it doesn't work — you get £2 in cash and your share is worth about £2 less. If a strategy sounds like free money in a market with millions of participants, assume it's already priced in.

Reinvesting: where the curve on the front page comes from

You can take dividends as cash, or automatically buy more shares with them. Do the second and those new shares pay dividends too, which buy more shares, which pay dividends. This is compounding, and over long periods reinvested dividends have accounted for a very large share of total stock market returns — in many long-run studies, roughly half or more.

It's also why the chart on the first tab bends upward instead of running in a straight line. Nothing dramatic happens in year three. A great deal happens in year thirty.

Three things to keep in mind

Dividends are not guaranteed
They're a decision, not a contract. Many were cut or suspended in 2020, including from companies that had paid for decades.
They're usually taxable
Rules vary enormously by country, and tax-sheltered accounts often exist for exactly this. Worth checking yours before you're surprised.
A dividend doesn't make a bad company good
Being paid 6% a year by a business losing 20% of its value is not winning.

The other half of the market

Bonds

Shares make you an owner. Bonds make you a lender. Almost nobody finds bonds exciting, which is roughly the point of them.

How a bond works

A government or company needs to borrow. It issues a bond: you hand over money now, it pays you a fixed rate of interest for an agreed period, and on the final date it returns your original sum. That's it. You have no ownership and no vote — you're a creditor, meaning someone the company owes money to.

Face value (or par)
The sum repaid at the end, often £100 or £1,000 per bond.
Coupon
The interest rate paid, usually annually or twice a year. The name comes from paper bonds with tear-off slips.
Maturity
The date you get your money back. Can be months or thirty-plus years.
Yield
Your actual return given what you paid — which is often not face value, because bonds are traded second-hand like shares.

The thing about bonds that isn't obvious

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. It sounds like finance trivia and it's the whole game.

Say you own a bond paying 2%. Interest rates then rise and new bonds are issued paying 5%. Nobody wants your 2% bond at full price when they can have 5% instead — so if you want to sell before maturity, you must accept less. Your bond's price falls until its effective return matches what's available elsewhere. It works in reverse too: when rates fall, existing higher-paying bonds become more valuable.

This is why 2022 was so unpleasant for cautious investors. Rates rose sharply, and bonds — the supposedly safe part — fell hard at the same time as shares.

Three risks, because "safe" is doing a lot of work

Default risk
The borrower doesn't pay. Rare for stable governments, real for companies.
Interest rate risk
Rates rise, your bond's market value falls. Only bites if you sell before maturity.
Inflation risk
The quiet one. A fixed 3% is a loss in purchasing power when inflation is 6%. Your money comes back intact and buys less.

Why anyone holds them

Bonds usually return less than shares over long periods. What they offer instead is predictability, an income stream, and the tendency — not the guarantee — to hold up better when share markets fall. That's why portfolios often mix the two, and why the mix typically shifts toward bonds as someone nears the point of actually needing the money. A 19-year-old with forty years ahead and a 64-year-old retiring next spring have genuinely different problems.

Exchanges and indexes

The places, and the scoreboards

These two get mixed up constantly, including by the news. Sorting them out takes one paragraph and makes market coverage suddenly readable.

An exchange is a venue. An index is a number.

The London Stock Exchange is a marketplace where shares change hands — a place. The FTSE 100 is a calculation that tracks 100 of the companies listed there — a scoreboard. You can buy shares on an exchange. You cannot buy an index, only a fund that copies it. So "the Nasdaq fell today" is doing double duty, because Nasdaq is both an exchange and an index that shares its name. The S&P 500 is only an index; it isn't a place at all.

Major exchanges

New York Stock Exchange
New York · founded 1792
Exchange

The largest exchange in the world by the total value of the companies listed on it, and the home of a great many long-established American businesses. Still has a physical trading floor, though nearly all real trading is electronic — the floor now serves mainly as a broadcast backdrop.

Nasdaq
New York · founded 1971
Exchange

The first fully electronic stock market, with no trading floor by design, and second only to the NYSE by the total value of the companies listed on it. Heavily weighted toward technology, which makes it more volatile than the NYSE — it rose furthest in the dot-com boom and fell furthest afterwards.

London Stock Exchange
London · roots to 1698
Exchange

One of the oldest exchanges still running, descended from brokers meeting in coffee houses. Europe's most internationally weighted market, with a large share of listings in energy, mining, banking and pharmaceuticals rather than technology.

Japan Exchange Group
Tokyo · exchange from 1878, group from 2013
Exchange

Japan's main exchange and one of the largest in Asia, formed when the Tokyo and Osaka exchanges merged in 2013. Japan's market spent decades below its 1989 peak — a useful corrective to anyone who assumes markets always recover quickly.

Johannesburg Stock Exchange
Johannesburg · founded 1887
Exchange

Africa's largest exchange, created to fund the mining companies of the Witwatersrand gold rush. Still weighted toward mining and resources alongside major banks and retailers, and closely watched as a gauge of the wider African economy.

Shanghai & Hong Kong
China · 1990 and 1891
Exchanges

Shanghai is the largest exchange in Asia by the total value of the companies listed on it. Hong Kong has long been the main route for international investors into Chinese companies; mainland exchanges have historically had tighter restrictions on foreign participation.

Major indexes

S&P 500
United States · from 1957
Index

500 of the largest US public companies, weighted by size — so the biggest handful influence it far more than the smallest. Widely treated as the default measure of the American market, and the most commonly tracked index in the world.

FTSE 100
United Kingdom · from 1984
Index

The 100 largest companies on the London Stock Exchange by market value, known as the "Footsie". It started at 1,000 points in January 1984. A quirk worth knowing: most of its members earn the majority of their revenue abroad, so it often reacts more to the pound and global commodity prices than to the UK economy.

Dow Jones Industrial Average
United States · from 1896
Index

Just 30 large US companies, and the oldest index still quoted. It's price-weighted, meaning a company with a high share price sways it more than a company that's simply bigger — an approach almost nobody would design today. Famous rather than representative.

Nasdaq Composite
United States · from 1971
Index

Tracks essentially everything listed on Nasdaq — thousands of companies, heavily tilted toward technology. When headlines say tech had a bad day, this is usually the number they mean.

Nikkei 225
Japan · from 1950
Index

225 major Japanese companies. Also price-weighted, like the Dow. Its 1989 peak and the long decline that followed is the standard case study in what a bubble can cost a country.

JSE Top 40 / All Share
South Africa · from 2002
Index

Both launched in their current form in June 2002, though the exchange had its own indexes long before that. The Top 40 tracks the largest companies on the Johannesburg exchange; the All Share covers a far broader sweep of the market. Both lean heavily on mining and resources, so they move with global commodity prices as much as with domestic news.

MSCI World / FTSE All-World
Global · since the late 1960s
Index

Thousands of companies across dozens of countries in a single number. Less famous than the others and arguably more useful, because funds tracking these are how an ordinary person owns a slice of the entire world economy in one purchase.

How trading actually happens now

Almost none of it looks like the films. Orders are matched by computers in milliseconds, and a large share of volume comes from algorithms rather than people. Exchanges have opening hours — London roughly 8am to 4.30pm, New York 9.30am to 4pm local time — which is why a company can announce disastrous news at 9pm and its shares only fall the next morning.

Most exchanges also have circuit breakers: automatic pauses that halt trading if prices fall too far too fast. They were introduced after 1987 to give humans a moment to think, and they were triggered several times during the March 2020 crash.

Basic strategies and trends

What people actually do with all this

This is education rather than a recommendation — you'd need to know someone's situation to recommend anything. But these are the ideas that come up repeatedly, and knowing them makes you much harder to sell nonsense to.

Before any of it: the boring order of operations

Almost every serious source agrees on roughly this sequence, and it's dull enough that finance influencers skip it entirely.

1

Clear expensive debt first

Credit cards typically charge 20–40% a year, and payday loans are far worse — a typical UK payday loan works out at well over 1,000% a year once the fees are annualised. No investment reliably beats those rates, so clearing that debt is a guaranteed return you cannot get anywhere else.

2

Build a cash buffer

Three to six months of essential costs, in an ordinary savings account. This exists so that a broken laptop doesn't force you to sell investments at the worst possible moment.

3

Only then invest money you won't need for years

Money needed within five years generally shouldn't be in the stock market. Over short periods it's close to a coin toss, and coin tosses are a bad way to fund a deposit.

The ideas worth understanding

Diversification
Spreading money across many companies, industries and countries so no single failure is fatal. The one thing in finance that genuinely reduces risk without reducing expected return — which is why it gets called the only free lunch.
Time in the market, not timing the market
Trying to sell before falls and buy before rises requires being right twice, repeatedly. Studies consistently find that missing just the best handful of days in a decade guts long-term returns — and the best days cluster suspiciously close to the worst ones, during the exact panics that tempt people to sell.
Cost averaging
Investing a fixed sum on a schedule regardless of the price. You automatically buy more when things are cheap and less when they're expensive, and — more importantly — you remove the decision, which is where most people go wrong.
Passive vs active
Passive means tracking a whole market cheaply. Active means paying someone to pick. Over long periods, the large majority of active funds have failed to beat their benchmark — the index they measure themselves against — after fees. That's not a moral claim about fund managers; it's arithmetic about costs.
Asset allocation
The split between shares, bonds and cash. This decision has historically explained far more of a portfolio's outcome than which specific companies were chosen, and it gets a fraction of the attention.
Risk tolerance
Two halves: how much loss you can financially absorb, and how much you can emotionally tolerate without selling. The second is the one people overestimate, invariably during a bull market.
Fees are the most underrated force in this entire subject

An annual fee of 1.5% instead of 0.2% sounds trivial. Compounded across forty years it can consume a very large fraction of what you would otherwise have ended up with — frequently more than a third. Fees come off every year, in good years and bad, and they're the one variable you can control with total certainty. Check the ongoing charge on anything before you buy it.

Trends you'll hear about

Index funds and ETFs
The dominant shift of the last few decades. Enormous sums have moved from expensive stock-picking funds into cheap trackers, largely because the evidence favoured it.
Commission-free apps
Trading is now free and instant on your phone. Genuinely democratising, and genuinely a design that encourages overtrading — which research links to worse returns. Both things are true.
Fractional shares
Buying part of a share. The single biggest practical reason "you need thousands to start" stopped being true.
ESG and ethical investing
Screening investments on environmental and social grounds. Growing fast, and fairly criticised for inconsistent standards and "greenwashing" — funds labelled ethical that hold much the same things as ordinary ones.
Finfluencers
Financial advice on social media. Some is excellent. A lot is undisclosed promotion, and several regulators have started acting against it. Assume there's an incentive until shown otherwise.
Three things a beginner should treat as off-limits

Leverage and CFDs (contracts for difference, which let you bet on price moves using borrowed money) — borrowing to invest multiplies losses as well as gains, and brokers are legally required to publish how many of their ordinary customers lose money. The figures are commonly around 70–80%. Day trading — repeated academic studies of active retail traders find that the large majority lose money over time. Anything you can't explain to a friend in two sentences.

Risks, pitfalls and fraud

How people actually lose money

Not usually by choosing a slightly worse company. By panicking, by paying too much in fees, and by being deliberately targeted. Young people with small accounts and social media habits are a favoured target, not an ignored one.

Pump and dump, in four stages

A pump and dump is a fraud where organisers buy a small, cheap, thinly traded asset, hype it until strangers pile in, then sell into that demand and leave everyone else holding it. It works on penny stocks — shares in tiny companies that trade for a few pence each — and on crypto tokens, and it has worked in essentially this form since the 1700s. Only the platform changes.

1

Accumulate, quietly

Organisers buy up a large share of something obscure — a company worth very little, or a newly minted token. It must be small and thinly traded, because that's what lets a modest amount of buying move the price a lot.

2

Pump

Coordinated hype. Group chats, short videos, screenshots of profits, a story about a merger or a breakthrough. The message is always urgency: it's going parabolic, you're early, don't miss it. Real buyers arrive and the price climbs, which becomes the proof that the hype was right.

3

Dump

The organisers sell everything into the demand they created. Because they hold so much, their selling breaks the price. This happens over hours, sometimes minutes.

4

The floor falls out

The price collapses, usually below where it started. The group chat goes quiet or pivots to "hold the line". Latecomers can't sell at any sensible price because nobody's buying. The organisers have already moved on to the next one.

Warning signs, in rough order of how reliable they are
  • Urgency. Any pressure to act now, before you've had time to look into it. Legitimate investments are still there next week.
  • Guaranteed or specific returns. "10x by Friday", "guaranteed 20% monthly". Nobody can guarantee market returns. Nobody.
  • A tiny, obscure asset. If you can't find independent coverage from a source that isn't promoting it, that's the setup.
  • Recruitment. You're encouraged to bring friends in. Ask why a working method needs more buyers.
  • Screenshots as evidence. Profit screenshots are trivial to fake and always show the wins.
  • Anonymous or unverifiable people. No real name, no regulated firm, no address.
  • Off-platform contact. Moving you to a private group, Telegram, WhatsApp or DMs — away from anywhere the claims might be challenged or recorded.
  • Contempt for questions. Being told you're negative, poor-minded or "not going to make it" for asking how it works. Real explanations survive questions.

Related frauds using the same machinery

Ponzi scheme
Returns paid to early investors out of later investors' deposits rather than any real profit. Requires constant new money and collapses when it stops. The tell is unusually steady returns in every market condition.
Rug pull
The crypto version. Developers launch a token, gather funds, then drain the liquidity and disappear, leaving something that can't be sold.
Signals and course selling
Paying monthly for trade tips or a "system". The reliable money is in the subscription, not the trades. Ask to see audited results rather than screenshots.
Impersonation and fake platforms
Cloned websites of real firms, or fabricated brokers showing fake profits — which let you withdraw a small amount early to build trust, then demand fees before a larger withdrawal that never arrives.
Recovery scams
Someone contacts a victim offering to recover lost funds for an upfront fee. Frequently the same people, working the list a second time.
The check that takes two minutes

Every serious market has a regulator with a public register of authorised firms, and most publish a warning list of companies to avoid. Search the firm's name on the regulator's own site — not on a link the firm gave you. In the UK that's the Financial Conduct Authority, in the US the SEC and FINRA, in South Africa the Financial Sector Conduct Authority, in Australia ASIC. If a firm isn't on the register, you have your answer, and if something goes wrong with an unregulated firm you will usually have no protection at all.

The pitfalls that aren't fraud

Most money lost isn't stolen. It's given away through ordinary human behaviour, and knowing the names helps you catch yourself.

Panic selling
Selling during a crash converts a temporary paper loss into a permanent real one, and people who do it rarely buy back in before the recovery. This is the single most expensive habit on the list.
FOMO buying
Buying because something has already risen a great deal, which by definition means paying more than everyone who's now selling to you.
Overconfidence
A few early wins in a rising market feel like skill. They're usually a rising market. This is where people start borrowing.
Loss aversion
Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please us, so people hold failing investments hoping to "get back to even" while selling their winners early — precisely backwards.
Survivorship bias
You see the person who put everything into one stock and won. You don't see the thousands who did the same and lost, because they don't post about it.
Concentration
Everything in one company, one sector, or one country. It feels like conviction right up until it doesn't.
If you think you've been scammed

Stop sending money immediately, including any "release fee" or "tax" needed to get your funds out — that's part of the same fraud. Contact your bank, which may be able to halt or recover a recent payment. Report it to your national regulator and police fraud service. Keep every message and screenshot. And tell someone you trust: shame is what scammers rely on to keep victims quiet, and it's the reason so much of this goes unreported. Being defrauded by professionals is not a character flaw.

Why the professionals aren't much better

This is the uncomfortable part. Large banks publish year-end targets for major indexes every January, and journalists have made a running joke of comparing them to what actually happened — the misses are routinely enormous, and in the years that mattered most, like 2008 and 2020, almost nobody's forecast was close. Economists' record on predicting recessions is similarly poor; the large majority of recessions in recent decades were not forecast a year ahead by the consensus.

It isn't that these people are stupid or lazy. It's that they're being asked to do something that may not be possible. A market price already contains everyone's collective best guess about the future. To beat it consistently you need to be reliably more right than the combined judgement of millions of people, many of whom are extremely well resourced. Some manage it for a while. Very few manage it for decades, and identifying them in advance is its own unsolved problem.

Long-term and short-term are different questions

"Nobody can predict the market" is shorthand, and it's only true over short periods. Nobody can tell you what next Tuesday holds. But it is reasonable to say that over thirty years the global economy will probably be larger than it is today, and that owning a slice of it will probably have beaten cash. That's not a forecast of a number, it's a statement about direction over a long stretch — and it's the difference between investing and predicting.

How to judge anyone's prediction

You'll never stop encountering forecasts, so the useful skill is grading them. Five questions, in order of how much they reveal.

Is it falsifiable?
"The market will correct" is unfalsifiable — no number, no date, so it can never be wrong. "The FTSE 100 will close lower on 31 December than it is today" can be checked. Vagueness is usually a choice.
What's their track record — all of it?
Anyone can show their best calls. Ask what share of their predictions came true, including the ones they've stopped mentioning. Someone who called one crash correctly has probably also called nine that never came.
What do they gain if you believe it?
Do they own the thing they're recommending? Sell a course, a newsletter, a signals group? Get paid by views? An incentive doesn't make someone wrong, but it tells you how hard to check.
Is there a time frame?
A prediction with no deadline can be defended forever with "just wait". Every genuine forecast has an expiry date attached.
How confident are they, really?
Careful forecasters speak in probabilities and say when they'd change their mind. Total certainty about an uncertain system is a warning sign, not a sign of expertise.

Your prediction log

Hindsight bias is the tendency to remember having known all along. It's near-universal, and the only real defence is writing the prediction down before the outcome. Log a few here — about markets, a company, an election, anything checkable — then revisit them on the date you set.

    Where to go next

    Websites, videos and books

    This site is an introduction, not an education. Everything below goes deeper. It's ordered by how much you can trust it, which is not the same as how entertaining it is.

    Before you click anything: who is paying for this?

    Financial content is unusual in that most of it is free, which means you are rarely the customer. Regulators publish material because their job is to stop people being defrauded. Brokers publish material to get you trading, because they earn when you do. Influencers publish because attention pays, and the most extreme claim gets the most attention. None of that makes a source useless — it tells you which direction to expect the bias to point.

    Regulators and public bodies

    The least exciting and most reliable material on the internet. These organisations have no product to sell you, and several run free scam-checking tools.

    Investor.gov — US Securities and Exchange CommissionFree

    Plain-English explanations of nearly every term on this site, plus a compound interest calculator and a search tool for checking whether an adviser is registered.

    MoneyHelper — UK, government-backedFree

    Covers the whole picture rather than just investing: budgeting, debt, pensions and savings. The best starting point if you're in the UK and the word "investing" still feels several steps away.

    FCA ScamSmart — UK Financial Conduct AuthorityFree

    The register of authorised firms and the warning list of ones to avoid. This is the two-minute check described on the Risks tab.

    FINRA Investor Education — USFree

    Strong material on fraud, plus BrokerCheck for looking up the history and disciplinary record of any US broker.

    FSCA — Financial Sector Conduct Authority, South AfricaFree

    The South African regulator. Publishes warnings about unlicensed operators and lets you verify whether a financial services provider is authorised.

    Reference and courses

    Khan Academy — Finance and Capital MarketsFree

    A structured video course from basic interest through to how the 2008 crisis actually worked, taught patiently and assuming nothing. Probably the single best free course on this subject.

    InvestopediaFree

    The default dictionary of finance. Excellent for looking up a term you've just met. Be aware that some pages carry broker advertising, so treat the reference articles as stronger than the recommendation ones.

    Bogleheads WikiFree

    A volunteer community built around low-cost index investing. Opinionated in a clearly stated direction, unusually evidence-driven, and completely uninterested in selling you anything. Has country-specific guides beyond the US.

    Morningstar

    Independent research and, crucially, fee data on funds. Useful when you want to check what a fund actually charges rather than what its marketing says.

    Video channels

    Search these names rather than following links from ads or comments — impersonation accounts are common on financial content. Rough test for any channel: does it ever say "I don't know", and does it show losses as well as wins?

    The Plain Bagel

    Made by a Canadian analyst. Calm explainers of concepts and unusually good breakdowns of specific scams and collapses, without any hype.

    Ben Felix / The Rational Reminder

    Short videos summarising actual academic research on investing, with citations. Denser than most, and the closest thing to a peer-reviewed YouTube channel on this topic.

    Patrick Boyle

    A finance professor and former fund manager. Dryly funny explanations of how markets work and detailed post-mortems of frauds, which pairs well with the Risks tab.

    Aswath Damodaran

    An NYU professor who posts his full university valuation lectures for free. Genuinely advanced — worth knowing exists for when you want to go much deeper.

    Two Cents (PBS) and CrashCourse Economics

    Short, made for people starting from zero, and pitched at roughly school age without being condescending. Good for the fundamentals underneath investing.

    Podcasts

    Planet Money — NPR

    Twenty-minute episodes that explain an economic idea through a story. Frequently the easiest way into a concept that reads as dry on a page.

    Odd Lots — Bloomberg

    Long interviews about what is actually happening in markets right now. Harder going, and a good next step once the vocabulary on this site feels comfortable.

    The Rational Reminder

    Evidence-based investing at length, including interviews with the academics whose research the rest of the field quotes.

    Books, if you want one thing that lasts

    The Psychology of Money — Morgan Housel

    Short essays on why people behave irrationally with money. The best first book on this list, because behaviour matters more than technique and it's readable in a weekend.

    The Little Book of Common Sense Investing — John Bogle

    The case for cheap index funds, argued by the man who created the first one. Repetitive by design, and the repetition is the point.

    A Random Walk Down Wall Street — Burton Malkiel

    The classic argument that short-term price movements are close to unpredictable — the idea underneath the Predictions tab. Regularly updated since 1973.

    The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham

    First published in 1949 and still cited constantly. Dated in its examples and demanding to read, but the chapters on market psychology have aged remarkably well.

    Films and documentaries

    Inside Job (2010)

    Documentary on the causes of the 2008 crisis. Has a clear argument it wants you to accept, so watch it as one well-made case rather than the neutral account.

    The Big Short (2015)

    A drama, so it compresses and simplifies — but it explains mortgage-backed securities better than most textbooks and is honest about how few people saw it coming.

    Dumb Money (2023)

    The GameStop story from the History tab. Entertaining, and worth watching alongside reporting on the people who bought near the top and lost.

    What to be careful with

    Content promising specific returns, "signals" or copy-trading groups, anything that needs you to sign up through a referral link, and short-form videos showing profit screenshots. Referral links mean the creator is paid when you open an account, which is disclosed far less often than the rules require. A useful habit: before acting on any financial content, look for the same claim on a regulator's site. If it isn't there, ask why.
